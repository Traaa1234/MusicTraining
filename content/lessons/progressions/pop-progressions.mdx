# Pop Progressions — I–V–vi–IV and Friends

Jazz leans on the ii–V–I. Popular music leans on something even simpler: a
handful of **four-chord loops** that repeat for an entire song. A few of these
loops underpin a startling share of the pop, rock and folk you have ever
heard.

## The four-chord song

The most famous of all is **I–V–vi–IV**. In the key of C major:

`C – G – Am – F`

Play that loop round and round and you have the harmonic skeleton of countless
hit songs across every decade. Comedians have built entire routines out of
medleys proving how many songs share these exact four chords.

## Why this loop works so well

Three of its four chords — **I, V and IV** — are the bright primary chords from
the Chords track. Dropped into the middle is **vi**, the relative minor. That
single minor chord is the secret: it lets a cheerful major-key loop dip into a
moment of wistfulness without ever leaving the key.

The loop also never fully rests. It touches home at the I, drifts through
tension and the minor colour, and arrives at IV — which leans straight back
toward I as the loop restarts. It is a circle with no real end, perfect for a
verse or chorus that repeats.

## The same four chords, rotated

Here is the trick that multiplies these loops: you can **start the same four
chords on a different chord** and get a new progression with a new mood.

| Progression | In C major | Feel |
| --- | --- | --- |
| I–V–vi–IV | C – G – Am – F | bright, anthemic |
| vi–IV–I–V | Am – F – C – G | yearning, emotional |
| I–vi–IV–V | C – Am – F – G | nostalgic "doo-wop" |
| IV–I–V–vi | F – C – G – Am | uplifting, building |

They draw on the same four chords, yet starting on **vi** (Am) opens a song in
shadow, while starting on **I** opens it in sunlight. Same ingredients,
different first impression.

## The 50s progression

One rotation deserves its own name. **I–vi–IV–V** — C–Am–F–G — is the **"50s
progression"**, the doo-wop loop behind a generation of ballads.

## Three chords are enough too

Strip away even the vi and you reach the oldest loop of all: **I–IV–V**, the
three-chord progression behind folk songs, punk, country and early rock and
roll. Three major chords, endless songs. Popular music has never needed many
chords — it needs the *right* few, looped with conviction.

## Why it matters

Pop progressions show the other face of harmony from the ii–V–I: not a journey
that resolves, but a **loop that sustains**. Learn these four-chord patterns and
their rotations and you can play along with — or write — a huge slice of
popular music. Next, the final lesson covers the 12-bar blues form and how to
spice any progression with **borrowed chords**.
