# The ii–V–I — Harmony's Home Run

If the authentic cadence is how music ends, the **ii–V–I** is how it gets
there in style. It is the single most common progression in jazz and a
cornerstone of pop, and once you can hear it you will hear it constantly.

## Three chords, one journey

The ii–V–I is exactly what its numerals say: the **ii** chord, then the **V**
chord, then the **I** chord. In the key of C major those are:

`Dm7 – G7 – Cmaj7`

<PlayButton notes="D4 F4 A4 C5" label="ii — Dm7 (setting off)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G3 B3 D4 F4" label="V — G7 (maximum tension)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 B4" label="I — Cmaj7 (home)" chord />

The ii–V–I tells a tiny three-act story: **ii** steps gently away from home,
**V** raises the tension to its peak, and **I** resolves it. It is the
authentic cadence from the last lesson, with the ii chord added as a running
start.

## Why it works — root motion by fifths

Look at the roots of the three chords: **D, G, C**. Each one is a perfect
fifth above the next, or — the same thing — a fifth *down*: D down to G, G down
to C.

Motion by descending fifths is the strongest, most satisfying root movement in
harmony. It is the engine of the circle of fifths. The ii–V–I is two of those
steps chained together, which is why it feels like it is being pulled home by
gravity.

## Why it works — the tritone resolves

There is a second engine inside the ii–V–I, and it is the reason the V chord
does the heavy lifting.

The V chord here is a **dominant 7th** — G7 — and you know from the Chords
track that a dominant 7th hides a **tritone** between its 3rd and 7th. In G7
that tritone is **B and F**.

A tritone is unstable; it craves resolution. When G7 moves to Cmaj7, those two
notes each slide a half step onto stable tones:

- **B rises to C** — the leading tone pulling into the tonic
- **F falls to E** — settling onto the third of the I chord

<PlayButton notes="B3 F4" label="The tritone inside G7 — unstable" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4" label="...resolving outward into Cmaj7" chord />

Two voices, each moving a half step in opposite directions, lock the chord
home. That tiny squeeze is the sound of resolution itself.

## ii–V–i in a minor key

The ii–V–I has a minor-key form too, and two chords change:

`iim7♭5 – V7 – im7`

In C minor that is **Dm7♭5 – G7 – Cm7**.

<PlayButton notes="D4 F4 Ab4 C5" label="ii — Dm7♭5 (half-diminished)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G3 B3 D4 F4" label="V7 — G7" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 G4 Bb4" label="i — Cm7" chord />

The ii chord becomes **half-diminished** (the darker, searching colour from the
Chords track), and the I chord becomes minor. The V chord, though, stays a
**dominant 7th** — it keeps its B natural, a leading tone borrowed from
harmonic minor, because without that tritone the resolution would lose its
pull.

## The two forms compared

| Key | ii | V | I |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major | iim7 | V7 | Imaj7 |
| Minor | iim7♭5 | V7 | im7 |

The V is the constant. Major or minor, the dominant 7th and its tritone are
what drive the music home.

## Why it matters

The ii–V–I is the most efficient resolution in tonal music — root motion by
fifths and a tritone collapsing inward, working together. Whole jazz tunes are
strings of ii–V–I's in different keys. Spot one and you instantly know where
"home" is. Next, the lessons turn from this jazz workhorse to the four-chord
loops that power popular music.
