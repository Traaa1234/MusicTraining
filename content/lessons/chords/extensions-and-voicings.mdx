# Extensions & Voicings

You can stack thirds past the seventh, and you can rearrange the notes you
already have. The first gives you **extensions** — richer colour. The second
gives you **voicings** — the same chord wearing different clothes. Together
they are the difference between a chord that is correct and a chord that
*sounds* good.

## Extensions — keep stacking thirds

A triad reaches the 5th. A seventh chord reaches the 7th. Keep adding thirds
and you pass the octave into the **extensions**: the **9th**, the **11th** and
the **13th**.

These numbers continue past 7 instead of resetting, because the notes really
do sit an octave higher than their plain-scale counterparts:

| Extension | Same note as | Example over C |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 9th | the 2nd, an octave up | D |
| 11th | the 4th, an octave up | F |
| 13th | the 6th, an octave up | A |

Add a 9th to a C dominant 7th and you get **C9**:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 Bb4 D5" label="C9 — a dominant 7th plus the 9th" chord />

Pile on the 13th as well and the chord glows:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 Bb4 D5 A5" label="C13 — fuller still" chord />

The key idea: an extension **adds colour without changing the chord's job**. A
C9 still behaves like a dominant chord — it just has more shimmer. Extensions
decorate; they do not redefine.

## Voicings — the same chord, rearranged

A chord is a *set of notes*, not a fixed shape. A **voicing** is one particular
way of arranging that set — which note is on the bottom, how the notes are
spaced, how high or low they sit. Change the voicing and the chord keeps its
name but changes its character.

### Inversions

The simplest voicing choice is **which note goes in the bass**. A C major chord
can put C, E or G on the bottom:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="Root position — C in the bass" chord />
<PlayButton notes="E4 G4 C5" label="First inversion — E in the bass" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G4 C5 E5" label="Second inversion — G in the bass" chord />

Same three notes, same chord — but each **inversion** has a slightly different
weight. Inversions let a bass line move smoothly while the harmony stays put.

### Open and closed

A **closed** voicing packs the notes as tightly as possible inside one octave.
An **open** voicing spreads them out across a wider range — and that spacing
sounds airier and less muddy, especially in the low register.

### Dropping and doubling

Real players rarely play every note of a big chord. They **drop** notes that
add little and **double** notes that anchor. The first note to go is usually
the **5th** — it is so stable it adds almost no colour. What you protect are
the **3rd** and the **7th**: together those two are the **guide tones**, and
they alone are enough to state whether a chord is major, minor or dominant.

## Voice leading — the payoff

All of this serves one goal: moving between chords **smoothly**. **Voice
leading** is the craft of choosing voicings so that each note travels the
shortest possible distance to the next chord — often just a half step, or no
move at all.

Pick clumsy voicings and a progression lurches. Pick voicings where the guide
tones glide by step into the next chord and the same progression sounds
inevitable and professional. This is the bridge into the next track: **Chord
Progressions** is where these voicings get strung together into the cadences
and loops that carry real songs.

## Why it matters

Extensions give you colour; voicings give you craft. The same humble C–F–G you
first harmonised with bare triads can now be a glowing Cmaj9, a spread-out IV,
a V13 — voiced so every note slips gently to the next. That is the point where
theory turns into music that genuinely sounds good.
