# Triads — The Three-Note Chords

A **chord** is several notes sounding at once. The smallest chord that still
has a full identity is the **triad** — three notes — and almost all harmony you
will ever meet is built from triads or small additions to them.

## Stack two thirds

You already know the trick from the Intervals track: **chords are stacks of
thirds**. Take a root note, add a third on top, then add another third on top
of that. Three notes, two thirds — a triad.

The three notes get names by their distance from the root:

- the **root** — the note the chord is named after
- the **third** — decides major or minor
- the **fifth** — the outer note, usually a perfect fifth

## The four triad qualities

Each of the two stacked thirds can be major (4 half steps) or minor (3 half
steps). The four combinations give the four triad **qualities**.

### Major triad

A **major third** then a **minor third**. Bright and resolved.

### Minor triad

A **minor third** then a **major third** — the reverse stack. Dark and serious.

### Diminished triad

**Two minor thirds**. The outer interval shrinks to a tritone, so the chord
sounds tense and unstable — it wants to move.

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 Gb4" label="C diminished triad" chord />

### Augmented triad

**Two major thirds**. The outer interval stretches to an augmented fifth. The
chord sounds suspended and dreamlike, with no clear gravity.

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G#4" label="C augmented triad" chord />

## The pattern at a glance

| Triad | Lower third | Upper third | Fifth | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major | major | minor | perfect | bright, stable |
| Minor | minor | major | perfect | dark, stable |
| Diminished | minor | minor | diminished | tense, unstable |
| Augmented | major | major | augmented | floating, unsettled |

Notice how tidy this is. **Major and minor are mirror images** — the same two
thirds in the opposite order. Diminished and augmented are the two "matched"
stacks — same third twice — and both lose the steadying perfect fifth, which is
exactly why they sound restless.

## Root, third, fifth — the working parts

Once a triad is built, each note has a job:

- The **root** names the chord and anchors it.
- The **third** is the colour switch — major or minor lives here, and nowhere
  else.
- The **fifth** is the reinforcement. It is so stable it adds little character;
  in fact it is the first note players drop when a chord gets crowded.

That ranking matters later: when you cannot play every note of a big chord, you
keep the third (it carries the quality) and you let the fifth go.

## Why it matters

Two stacked thirds, four qualities — that is the entire vocabulary of basic
harmony. Every pop song, every hymn, every folk tune is mostly major and minor
triads. The next lesson takes these triads and builds one on **every note of a
scale**, which is where chords start to tell a story.
