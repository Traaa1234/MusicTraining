# Diatonic Chords — Harmonising the Scale

A single triad is just a colour. The real power of harmony appears when you
build a triad on **every note of a scale** — because the qualities that come
out are fixed, and they are the same in every key. Learn the pattern once and
you know the chords of all twelve major keys.

## Harmonising the scale

To **harmonise** a scale, you build a triad on each degree using *only the
notes of that scale*. Start on the degree, skip a note, skip a note again —
stack thirds, but stay inside the key.

Take C major — the white keys. On degree 1, stack C, E, G. On degree 2, stack
D, F, A. Carry on up all seven degrees:

| Degree | Triad notes | Quality | Chord |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | C – E – G | major | C |
| 2 | D – F – A | minor | Dm |
| 3 | E – G – B | minor | Em |
| 4 | F – A – C | major | F |
| 5 | G – B – D | major | G |
| 6 | A – C – E | minor | Am |
| 7 | B – D – F | diminished | B° |

You did not *choose* those qualities — they fell out of the scale. The half
steps in the major scale land in fixed places, so the triads come out major or
minor or diminished in a fixed pattern.

## The pattern is the same in every key

Here is the pattern, stripped of the key:

**major – minor – minor – major – major – minor – diminished**

That sequence holds for **every major scale**. Harmonise G major, or E♭ major,
or any of the twelve, and you always get major, minor, minor, major, major,
minor, diminished — in that order. Memorise the seven words and you have the
chords of every major key for free.

## Roman numerals

Musicians label these chords with **Roman numerals**, counting the scale
degree. The case of the numeral tells you the quality:

- **UPPERCASE** = major chord — I, IV, V
- **lowercase** = minor chord — ii, iii, vi
- **lowercase with °** = diminished chord — vii°

So the harmonised major scale is always:

`I – ii – iii – IV – V – vi – vii°`

The beauty of numerals is that they are **key-independent**. "I–V–vi–IV" is a
chord progression you can play in any key — in C it is C–G–Am–F, in G it is
G–D–Em–C. The numbers describe the *story*; the key just sets the pitch.

## The three primary chords

Three of the seven do most of the work: **I, IV and V** — the three major
chords, also called the **primary** or **tonic–subdominant–dominant** chords.

<PlayButton notes="F3 A3 C4" label="IV — F major (away)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G3 B3 D4" label="V — G major (tension, wants home)" chord />

Between them, I, IV and V can harmonise an enormous share of folk, blues and
pop. Each has a role: **I** is home, **V** is the tension that pulls back to
it, and **IV** is the gentle step away in between.

## vi — the relative minor

One more chord earns a special mention: **vi**. In C major that is **A minor**,
and A minor is the **relative minor** of C — the two scales share all seven
notes. The vi chord lets a major key borrow a touch of sadness without
actually leaving the key, which is why the I–V–**vi**–IV progression sounds so
bittersweet.

## Why it matters

Diatonic chords turn a scale into a **palette of seven related chords** that
all belong together and sound good in combination. Roman numerals let you carry
a progression from key to key. With this, you are ready for the next track —
**Chord Progressions** — where these numerals get arranged into the cadences
and chord loops that power real songs. First, though, the next lesson makes
each of these chords richer by adding a fourth note: the **seventh**.
