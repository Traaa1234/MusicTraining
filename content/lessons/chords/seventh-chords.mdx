# Seventh Chords

A triad has three notes. Stack **one more third** on top and you get a
**seventh chord** — a four-note chord with far more colour and pull. Sevenths
are the sound of jazz, soul, gospel and most sophisticated pop.

## One more third

You already stack thirds to build a triad: root, third, fifth. Keep going —
add a third above the fifth — and you reach the **seventh** of the chord. Now
the chord has four notes: **root, third, fifth, seventh**.

Just as the *third* decided major versus minor, the **seventh** decides the
flavour of a seventh chord. There are two sizes, exactly as with thirds:

- a **major seventh** — 11 half steps above the root
- a **minor seventh** — 10 half steps above the root

Mix the triad quality with the seventh quality and you get the family of
seventh chords below.

## The major seventh chord

A **major triad** plus a **major seventh**. Lush, warm, dreamy — the sound of a
soft landing.

<Fretboard chord="C major7" />

## The dominant seventh chord

A **major triad** plus a **minor seventh**. This is the restless one. Inside it
hides a **tritone** — between the chord's third and seventh — and you met that
tension back in the Intervals track. The tritone is why a dominant 7th sounds
like it *must* resolve somewhere.

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 Bb4" label="C dominant 7 (C7)" chord />

The dominant 7th is built on the **V** chord of a key, and its craving to
resolve down to **I** is the strongest pull in all of harmony.

## The minor seventh chord

A **minor triad** plus a **minor seventh**. Smooth, mellow, relaxed — minor
without the heaviness.

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 G4 Bb4" label="C minor 7 (Cm7)" chord />

## The two tense sevenths

Built on the diminished triad, two more sevenths complete the set:

The **half-diminished** chord — a diminished triad plus a *minor* seventh, also
written **m7♭5**:

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 Gb4 Bb4" label="C half-diminished (Cm7♭5)" chord />

The **fully diminished seventh** — a diminished triad plus a *diminished*
seventh. Every interval inside it is a minor third, which makes it eerily
symmetrical and very tense:

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 Gb4 A4" label="C diminished 7 (C°7)" chord />

## The five common seventh chords

| Chord | Symbol | Triad + 7th | Sound |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major seventh | maj7 | major triad + major 7th | lush, dreamy |
| Dominant seventh | 7 | major triad + minor 7th | tense, bluesy |
| Minor seventh | m7 | minor triad + minor 7th | smooth, mellow |
| Half-diminished | m7♭5 | diminished triad + minor 7th | dark, searching |
| Diminished seventh | °7 | diminished triad + diminished 7th | very tense, symmetrical |

## Harmonising the scale, again

Just as triads come from harmonising a scale, so do sevenths — add the seventh
to each diatonic triad. In a major key the result is fixed:

`Imaj7 – iim7 – iiim7 – IVmaj7 – V7 – vim7 – viiø7`

Notice that only **one** chord in the whole key is a dominant 7th: the **V7**.
That uniqueness is what makes the dominant 7th such a strong signpost — when
you hear one, your ear knows where home is.

## Why it matters

Seventh chords are where harmony stops being merely correct and starts being
**expressive**. The same I–IV–V you harmonised with plain triads becomes
something richer as Imaj7–IVmaj7–V7. The next lesson goes one step further —
stacking thirds *past* the seventh into **extensions**, and rearranging the
notes you have into **voicings**.
