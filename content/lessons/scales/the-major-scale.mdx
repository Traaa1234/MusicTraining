# The Major Scale

If you learn one scale in your whole life, learn this one. The **major scale**
is the reference ruler of Western music — every other scale, every chord, every
key is described as a change *from* it. Get it solid and the rest of theory has
a measuring stick.

## A formula, not a memory test

You met the recipe in the Fundamentals track. The major scale is a fixed
pattern of whole (**W**) and half (**H**) steps:

`W – W – H – W – W – W – H`

Start that pattern on C and it lands on every white key — no sharps, no flats:

<Piano scale="C major" />

<PlayButton notes="C4 D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 B4 C5" label="Hear C major, bottom to top" />

Eight notes, seven steps, and you arrive back home an octave higher. The two
**half steps** are the important part — they fall between steps 3–4 and 7–8.
Everywhere else is a whole step.

## The same formula on any note

Because it is a formula, you can start it anywhere. Start on **G** and follow
`W–W–H–W–W–W–H` and you are forced to use one black key — **F♯** — to make the
spacing come out right:

<Piano scale="G major" />

<PlayButton notes="G3 A3 B3 C4 D4 E4 F#4 G4" label="Hear G major" />

That single F♯ is *why* the key of G major has one sharp. Every key signature
in music is just the set of sharps or flats the major-scale formula demands.

## The scale degrees have names

Each step of the scale has a number — and a traditional name that tells you its
job:

| Degree | Name | Role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Tonic | home — the centre of gravity |
| 2 | Supertonic | one step above the tonic |
| 3 | Mediant | midway to the dominant; sets major mood |
| 4 | Subdominant | a gentle pull away from home |
| 5 | Dominant | the strongest note after the tonic |
| 6 | Submediant | the relative-minor centre |
| 7 | Leading tone | leans hard *up* into the tonic |

The two names worth remembering now are **tonic** (degree 1, home) and
**dominant** (degree 5, the most powerful pull back toward home).

## The leading tone

Degree 7 has a special magnetism. It sits only a **half step** below the tonic,
and that closeness makes it strain upward — which is exactly why it is called
the **leading tone**. Play degrees 7 and 8 and you can feel the resolution snap
shut:

<PlayButton notes="B4 C5" label="Leading tone resolving home" />

That tiny half step doing the pulling is the same half step from the formula.
The major scale sounds finished and bright largely because of it.

## Do, re, mi

You already know the major scale by another set of names — the **solfège**
syllables:

`do – re – mi – fa – sol – la – ti – do`

Singers use these to learn melodies without an instrument. *Do* is the tonic,
*ti* is the leading tone straining back up to *do*. Sing them once and you have
sung the major scale.

## Why it matters

The major scale is the origin point. Minor scales are described as "a major
scale with degrees lowered". Modes are "the major scale started from a
different degree". Chords are notes "1, 3 and 5 of a scale". Master this one
pattern and everything ahead is a small edit to something you already know.
