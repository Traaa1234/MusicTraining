# The Blues Scale

Take the minor pentatonic scale, add a single extra note, and you get the most
expressive scale in popular music: the **blues scale**. That one added note —
the **blue note** — carries almost the entire emotional weight of blues, rock,
soul and jazz.

## One note added to the minor pentatonic

You already know the minor pentatonic. In C it is:

`C – E♭ – F – G – B♭`

The blues scale slips **one chromatic note** in between the 4th (F) and the 5th
(G) — a note that is the **♭5**, also spellable as the ♯4. The full C blues
scale is six notes:

`C – E♭ – F – G♭ – G – B♭`

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 F4 Gb4 G4 Bb4 C5" label="Hear the C blues scale" />

That G♭ squeezed between F and G is the **blue note**.

## What the blue note does

The blue note is not a resting place — it is a note you **pass through**.
Listen to that little chromatic slither in the middle of the scale: F → G♭ → G.
Three notes a half step apart, sliding tension into release.

<PlayButton notes="F4 Gb4 G4" label="The chromatic slither — F, blue note, G" />

It works because it is the **tritone** against the tonic — the most unstable
interval you met in the Intervals track. Drop it in for a split second and the
ear *craves* the move back to a stable note. Land on it and lean, and you get
that crying, aching blues sound.

<Fretboard scale="C blues" />

## Major or minor? Both

Here is the strange magic of the blues. The blues scale is built from a *minor*
pentatonic, yet players use it freely over **major** chords — a dominant blues
in C plays C7, F7 and G7, all major-quality chords.

That clash of a minor-flavoured scale against major-flavoured chords is not a
mistake. It *is* the blues. The friction between the two is the genre's whole
identity — comfort and ache layered on top of each other.

| Scale | Notes | Compared with minor pentatonic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C minor pentatonic | C E♭ F G B♭ | — |
| C blues | C E♭ F G♭ G B♭ | adds the ♭5 blue note |

## Bending into the cracks

On a guitar, players rarely hit the blue note dead-on. They **bend** the string
— pushing the pitch up gradually from F toward G — so the note smears through
G♭ instead of stopping on it. Singers and harmonica players do the same with
pitches that live *between* the piano keys.

That is the deepest idea here: the true blue note is not really a fixed pitch
at all. It is a region — a bent, vocal, in-between zone. The G♭ on the keyboard
is just the closest a fixed-pitch instrument can get to it.

## Why it matters

The blues scale is your first scale built for **expression** rather than
structure. It teaches the lesson every improviser eventually needs: tension is
not something to avoid — placed and released with intent, it is the most
powerful tool you have. Every rock and blues solo you admire is mostly these
six notes, played with feel.
