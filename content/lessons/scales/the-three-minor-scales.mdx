# The Three Minor Scales

Major has one scale. Minor has **three** — and beginners often find this
confusing until they see *why*. The three minor scales are not three unrelated
things to memorise. They are one scale and two small repairs to a single
problem. This lesson walks through that problem and its fixes.

## Natural minor — the starting point

The **natural minor** scale is the plain minor scale. Its formula is:

`W – H – W – W – H – W – W`

Start it on C and you get C natural minor — three flats compared with C major:

<Piano scale="C natural-minor" />

<PlayButton notes="C4 D4 Eb4 F4 G4 Ab4 Bb4 C5" label="Hear C natural minor" />

Compared with the major scale, three degrees are lowered a half step — the
**3rd, 6th and 7th**. That flattened 3rd is what makes it sound minor; you met
it as the minor third back in the Intervals track.

## Natural minor's one weakness

Natural minor has a flaw. Look at its **7th degree** — in C natural minor that
is B♭. It sits a **whole step** below the tonic, not a half step.

Remember the leading tone from the major scale? It pulled strongly *up* into
the tonic because it was only a half step away. Natural minor's 7th is too far
away to pull like that. Play it and the ending sounds soft, unfinished — it
does not snap home.

<PlayButton notes="Bb4 C5" label="Natural minor 7th — a weak, soft ending" />

For centuries that was a problem: composers wanted minor-key music that could
still resolve with conviction. The other two minor scales are the two fixes.

## Harmonic minor — fix the 7th

The first fix is blunt: **raise the 7th degree by a half step.** That restores
a true leading tone, just a half step below the tonic.

<Piano scale="C harmonic-minor" />

<PlayButton notes="B4 C5" label="Harmonic minor 7th — a firm leading tone" />

Now the scale resolves with the same conviction as major. But the fix has a
side effect. Raising the 7th while leaving the 6th low opens a wide gap between
degrees **6 and 7** — three half steps, an **augmented second**:

<PlayButton notes="Ab4 B4" label="The augmented 2nd — harmonic minor's exotic leap" />

That stretched, almost Middle-Eastern-sounding leap is the signature of
harmonic minor. Useful when you want it, awkward when you are writing a smooth
melody.

## Melodic minor — fix the gap too

The second fix smooths that awkward gap. **Melodic minor raises both the 6th
*and* the 7th** while ascending — closing the augmented second back into
ordinary whole steps:

<Piano scale="C melodic-minor" />

<PlayButton notes="C4 D4 Eb4 F4 G4 A4 B4 C5" label="Hear C melodic minor ascending" />

Going *up*, melodic minor is almost identical to major — only the lowered 3rd
gives away that it is minor at all. In classical practice the raised 6th and
7th are only needed on the way up; coming back *down*, melody no longer needs
the upward pull, so it reverts to plain natural minor. (Jazz players often keep
the raised form in both directions and call it the "jazz minor".)

## The three side by side

| Scale | 3rd | 6th | 7th | Why use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Natural minor | ♭3 | ♭6 | ♭7 | plain minor; soft ending |
| Harmonic minor | ♭3 | ♭6 | **♮7** | strong resolution; exotic 6–7 leap |
| Melodic minor | ♭3 | **♮6** | **♮7** | strong resolution; smooth melody |

Read the table as a story: start with natural minor, raise the 7th to get
harmonic minor, then also raise the 6th to get melodic minor. One scale, two
repairs.

## Why it matters

Minor-key music constantly switches between these three forms — natural minor
for the general colour, harmonic or melodic minor at moments that need to
resolve. You do not pick one and stick to it; you draw the degree you need from
whichever form supplies it. Knowing all three means you can hear *why* a minor
melody bends the way it does.
