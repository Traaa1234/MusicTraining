# Major & Minor Thirds

The perfect fifth is solid but neutral — it tells you nothing about mood. The
**third** is the opposite. It is the single interval that decides whether music
sounds **happy or sad**, and learning to hear it is the biggest ear-training
win a beginner can score.

## What a third is

A **third** is the distance between a note and the note two letter names above
it: C up to E, D up to F, G up to B. Count the letters — C, D, E — and you land
on the third one. That is why it is called a third.

But "two letters up" comes in **two sizes**, and the difference is exactly one
half step. Those two sizes are the major third and the minor third.

## The major third — bright

A **major third** spans **four half steps**. From C, count up four semitones:
C → C♯ → D → D♯ → **E**.

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4" label="Hear a major third" />

It sounds open, confident, sunlit. You already know it by ear:

- The first two notes of *When the Saints Go Marching In*
- The opening of *Kumbaya*
- A bugle call — bugles can only play notes built from thirds and fifths

## The minor third — dark

A **minor third** spans **three half steps** — one semitone smaller. From C:
C → C♯ → D → **D♯/E♭**.

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4" label="Hear a minor third" />

It sounds softer, heavier, a little melancholy. Also deeply familiar:

- *Greensleeves* — "A-las, my love" leaps up a minor third
- The two-note cuckoo call — a minor third falling
- The opening riff of *Smoke on the Water*

## One half step is the whole story

Stack the two intervals side by side and the gap is tiny — a single key on the
piano — yet the emotional change is total.

| Interval | Half steps | From C | Mood |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Major third | 4 | C–E | bright, happy |
| Minor third | 3 | C–E♭ | dark, sad |

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4" label="Major third" />
<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4" label="Minor third" />

Play them back to back. That drop of one half step in the upper note is the
border between major and minor — the same border you met in the Fundamentals
track, now named as an interval.

## Thirds are the bricks of chords

Here is why thirds matter so much: **chords are stacks of thirds**. Take a root,
add a third, add another third on top, and you have a triad.

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="C major chord" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 G4" label="C minor chord" chord />

Both chords keep the same outer shell — C on the bottom, G on top, a perfect
fifth apart. Only the **middle note** moves, and that middle note is the third.
Major third on the bottom → major chord. Minor third on the bottom → minor
chord. Every triad you ever play is decided by this one interval.

You can see the stack on the keyboard. In C major the chord tones C, E and G
are every other white key — third, then third:

## Training your ear

Do not try to hear "a major third" as one lump of sound. Instead, sing the two
notes apart and ask one question: did the top note feel **lifted and bright**,
or **settled and dark**? That single yes/no judgement is all the third asks of
you — and with it you can label the mood of almost any chord you meet.
