# The Tritone & Interval Quality

One interval was missing a clear personality in the last lesson: the one at
**six half steps**, sitting exactly between the fourth and the fifth. It is the
**tritone** — the most unstable interval in music — and understanding it ties
together everything intervals can do.

## Exactly half an octave

A **tritone** spans **six half steps** — precisely half of the octave's twelve.

<PlayButton notes="C4 F#4" label="Hear a tritone" />

That perfect symmetry is the source of all its tension. Every other interval
leans somewhere; the tritone splits the octave dead even, so it leans
*nowhere* — it sounds restless, unsettled, almost asking a question.

Medieval theorists nicknamed it *diabolus in musica* — "the devil in music" —
and avoided it. Modern composers use it constantly:

- *Maria* from *West Side Story* — the name leaps up a tritone
- The opening two notes of *The Simpsons* theme
- The wailing of an emergency siren

## One sound, two names

The tritone is the clearest example of a rule you have already half-met:
**the same sound can be spelled two ways, and the spelling changes its name.**

- Counting *up* from C, F♯ is a raised fourth → an **augmented fourth**
- Counting *down* from C, G♭ is a lowered fifth → a **diminished fifth**

<PlayButton notes="C4 F#4" label="Augmented fourth (C–F♯)" />
<PlayButton notes="C4 Gb4" label="Diminished fifth (C–G♭)" />

Identical pitch, identical six half steps — yet one is a stretched fourth and
the other a shrunk fifth. Which name is correct depends on the key and the
chord around it.

## The five interval qualities

That gives us the full vocabulary. Every interval carries a **quality** word as
well as a number, and there are exactly five qualities:

| Quality | Means | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Perfect | the stable form | unisons, fourths, fifths, octaves |
| Major | the larger form | seconds, thirds, sixths, sevenths |
| Minor | a half step smaller than major | seconds, thirds, sixths, sevenths |
| Augmented | a half step *larger* than perfect or major | any interval |
| Diminished | a half step *smaller* than perfect or minor | any interval |

Notice the split. Fourths, fifths and octaves are never "major" or "minor" —
they are **perfect**. Seconds, thirds, sixths and sevenths are never "perfect"
— they are **major or minor**. Both groups can be pushed further out to
augmented, or pulled further in to diminished.

## Shrinking and stretching

Quality is just a sliding scale. Start from any interval and move one note by a
half step, and the quality shifts one notch:

`diminished → minor → major → augmented` (for thirds, sixths, sevenths)

`diminished → perfect → augmented` (for fourths, fifths)

So a major third made one half step smaller is a minor third; made one half
step smaller again it is a diminished third. A perfect fifth widened by a half
step is an augmented fifth; narrowed by a half step it is a diminished fifth —
which is, of course, our tritone again.

## Why the tension is useful

The tritone wants to **resolve**, and that craving is the engine of harmony.
In a dominant seventh chord — the chord that pulls hardest toward home — a
tritone hides between the third and the seventh:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 Bb4" label="C7 — listen for the tritone inside" chord />

The E and the B♭ in that chord are a tritone apart. When the chord resolves,
those two notes squeeze inward by a half step each onto stable tones. That is
why a dominant chord sounds like it *must* go somewhere — and you will meet it
again, in detail, in the Chords and Progressions tracks.

## Why it matters

With quality added to number, you can now name **any** interval exactly:
not just "a third" but "a *minor* third", not just "a fifth" but "a *diminished*
fifth". That precise vocabulary is what lets you spell every scale and every
chord from a formula — which is exactly where the lessons go next.
