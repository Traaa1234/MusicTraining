# Fourths, Sixths & Sevenths

You can already hear the octave, the fifth and the thirds. This lesson fills in
the rest of the diatonic intervals — the **fourth**, the **sixths** and the
**sevenths** — so that you can name every distance inside an octave by ear.

## The perfect fourth

A **perfect fourth** spans **five half steps** — from C up to F. Like the
fifth, it is called *perfect*: stable and clear, neither happy nor sad.

<PlayButton notes="C4 F4" label="Hear a perfect fourth" />

You know it instantly:

- *Here Comes the Bride* — the opening leap
- *Amazing Grace* — "A-ma-zing" rises a fourth
- The first two notes of *Auld Lang Syne*

The fourth has a slight forward lean — it sounds like it still wants to resolve,
where the fifth already sounds finished.

## The two sixths

A sixth is six letter names wide — C up to A. As with thirds, it comes in two
sizes a half step apart.

A **major sixth** spans **nine half steps**:

<PlayButton notes="C4 A4" label="Major sixth" />

Bright and leaping — the opening of *My Bonnie Lies Over the Ocean*, or the
old NBC three-note chime.

A **minor sixth** spans **eight half steps**:

<PlayButton notes="C4 Ab4" label="Minor sixth" />

Wistful and yearning — the famous theme from *Love Story*, or the upward reach
in *The Entertainer*.

## The two sevenths

Sevenths are the widest intervals before the octave, and the most tense — they
sit just one or two half steps short of "home".

A **major seventh** spans **eleven half steps** — one semitone shy of the
octave. It is the most strained interval of all, shimmering and unresolved:

<PlayButton notes="C4 B4" label="Major seventh" />

A **minor seventh** spans **ten half steps**. It is tense but warmer — think of
the leap in *Somewhere* from *West Side Story*, or the *Star Trek* theme:

<PlayButton notes="C4 Bb4" label="Minor seventh" />

## The full map of intervals

Here is every interval from the unison to the octave, with its size in half
steps. This table is worth memorising — it is the ruler you measure all music
with.

| Interval | Half steps | From C | Sounds |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Unison | 0 | C–C | same note |
| Minor second | 1 | C–D♭ | clashing |
| Major second | 2 | C–D | a step |
| Minor third | 3 | C–E♭ | dark |
| Major third | 4 | C–E | bright |
| Perfect fourth | 5 | C–F | open, leaning |
| Tritone | 6 | C–F♯ | unstable |
| Perfect fifth | 7 | C–G | open, solid |
| Minor sixth | 8 | C–A♭ | yearning |
| Major sixth | 9 | C–A | bright leap |
| Minor seventh | 10 | C–B♭ | tense, warm |
| Major seventh | 11 | C–B | strained |
| Octave | 12 | C–C | the same note, higher |

## Inversions: every interval has a partner

Flip an interval — move the lower note up an octave — and you get its
**inversion**. The two sizes always add up to **twelve**.

- A fourth (5) inverts to a fifth (7) — that is why they sound like cousins
- A major sixth (9) inverts to a minor third (3)
- A major seventh (11) inverts to a minor second (1)

The rule is tidy: major inverts to minor, perfect stays perfect, and the
numbers sum to twelve. Hear a wide interval as the "flip" of a narrow one and
half the work of recognising it is already done.
