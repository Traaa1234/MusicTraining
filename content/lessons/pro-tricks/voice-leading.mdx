# Voice Leading

You have spent eight tracks learning *which* notes and chords to play. This
final lesson is about something else entirely: playing them **beautifully**.
**Voice leading** is the craft of moving smoothly from one chord to the next —
and it is what separates harmony that is merely correct from harmony that
sounds professional.

## A chord is a set of voices

Think of a chord not as one block but as several independent **voices** — as
if a choir were singing it, one singer per note. Voice leading asks a simple
question: when the chord changes, how does *each singer* get to their next
note?

The clumsy answer is for everyone to leap. The professional answer follows two
rules.

## Rule 1 — keep common tones

If a note belongs to **both** the old chord and the new one, **keep it where it
is**. Do not move a voice that does not need to move.

C major (C–E–G) moving to A minor (A–C–E) shares **C and E**. Hold those two
voices still and only one voice has to move — G slips down to A:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="C major" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 A4" label="A minor — only G moved, down to A" chord />

Same A minor chord, but reached with the least possible motion. It sounds
seamless because almost nothing changed.

## Rule 2 — move the rest by step

When a voice *does* have to move, send it the **shortest distance** — a step,
or a half step, never a leap if a step will do.

The Chords track called the 3rd and 7th the **guide tones**. In a ii–V–I they
are perfectly built for this: voice the chords well and each guide tone slides
into the next chord by a single step. The G7 tritone you have met so many times
resolves *because* its two voices each move a half step — B up to C, F down to
E. That is voice leading doing its job.

## Contrary motion

One more professional habit: when voices move, let some go **up** while others
go **down**. Motion in opposite directions — **contrary motion** — sounds fuller
and more independent than every voice sliding the same way in parallel. It is
the difference between a choir and a single thickened line.

## The line cliché

Voice leading can become a feature in its own right. The **line cliché** is a
chord that stays put while **one inner voice walks chromatically**.

Hold a C major chord and let a single voice descend C – B – B♭ – A. The chord
barely changes, but that creeping line is unmistakable — you have heard it in
spy themes and torch songs for decades:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="C" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 B4" label="Cmaj7 — the line reaches B" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 Bb4" label="C7 — down to B♭" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 A4" label="C6 — down to A" chord />

Four chords, but really one chord with a melody hidden inside it. That is voice
leading turned into a hook.

## Smooth chords, smaller voicings

Good voice leading also shapes *how* you voice a chord. Rather than play every
note in root position and jump the whole shape around, players choose
**inversions** and drop notes so the move to the next chord is as small as
possible. Often the 5th is dropped and only the guide tones and root remain —
just enough to state the chord, positioned to glide.

## Why it matters — and the journey so far

Voice leading is the final polish. The same progression — even the same rich,
substituted, mixture-coloured progression from the last three lessons — can
lurch or can flow, entirely depending on how each voice travels.

And with that, the path is complete. You began with the names of seven notes.
You can now build scales and modes, stack chords and extensions, drive
progressions with cadences and the ii–V–I, place it all in time, write it in
any key — and now move between every chord with the smooth, deliberate craft of
a professional. Every one of these tools serves the same goal you started with:
training your ear, and making music that sounds *good*.
