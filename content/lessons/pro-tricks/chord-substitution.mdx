# Chord Substitution

**Chord substitution** is the art of replacing one chord with another that
does a similar job but wears a different colour. It is how a player takes a
plain progression and **reharmonizes** it — same melody, fresher chords.

## Chords have functions

Every diatonic chord belongs to one of three **functions** — three jobs a
chord can do in a key:

| Function | Job | Chords in a major key |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tonic | home, rest | I, iii, vi |
| Subdominant | move away | IV, ii |
| Dominant | tension, pull home | V, vii° |

The key idea: chords inside the **same function group can stand in for one
another**. Swap a chord for another with the same job and the progression's
logic survives — only its colour changes.

## Diatonic substitution

The simplest substitutions stay inside the key. Look at why I, iii and vi all
count as "tonic":

- **I** is C – E – G
- **iii** is E – G – B — it shares **E and G** with I
- **vi** is A – C – E — it shares **C and E** with I

Because they overlap by two notes, iii and vi sound like softer, shadier
versions of home.

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="I — C major (bright home)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="A3 C4 E4" label="vi — A minor (a shadier home)" chord />

Substituting **vi for I** is the most common move of all — it is exactly what
the I–V–**vi**–IV pop loop does. Reharmonizing is often nothing more than
choosing a same-function neighbour.

## The tritone substitution

The most striking substitution targets the **dominant** chord, and it is worth
a section of its own. It is called the **tritone substitution**.

Recall what powers a dominant 7th: the **tritone** inside it. In **G7** — G B D
F — that tritone is **B and F**.

Now here is the trick. Build the dominant 7th whose root is a **tritone away**
from G — that root is **D♭**, and the chord is **D♭7**: D♭ – F – A♭ – C♭. Its
own tritone? **F and C♭** — and C♭ is the same pitch as B.

**G7 and D♭7 contain the very same tritone.** That shared tritone is the part
that craves resolution — so D♭7 resolves to C just as convincingly as G7 does.

<PlayButton notes="Db4 F4 Ab4 B4" label="D♭7 — the tritone substitute" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="...both resolve to C" chord />

## Why the tritone sub sounds so good

The payoff is in the **bass line**. A normal ii–V–I in C moves its roots
D – G – C. Swap in the tritone sub and the roots become **D – D♭ – C** — a
smooth chromatic slide, each step a half step down:

<PlayButton notes="D4 F4 A4 C5" label="ii — Dm7" chord />
<PlayButton notes="Db4 F4 Ab4 B4" label="♭II7 — D♭7 (tritone sub for G7)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4 B4" label="I — Cmaj7" chord />

That chromatic descent is one of the most elegant sounds in jazz, and it costs
nothing — the chord still does the dominant's job.

## Substitution is reharmonization

String these ideas together and you can **reharmonize** a whole tune: keep the
melody, keep the underlying functions, but replace chords with same-function
neighbours, tritone subs and the secondary dominants from the last lesson. The
melody stays recognisable while the harmony underneath turns rich and personal.
Two musicians can play the same song and harmonise it completely differently —
that freedom is chord substitution.

## Why it matters

Substitution is where a player stops merely *playing* a progression and starts
*arranging* it. Knowing which chords share a function — and that any dominant
has a tritone twin — turns every progression into raw material you can recolour
at will. Next, the lessons reach outside the key entirely, with modal mixture.
