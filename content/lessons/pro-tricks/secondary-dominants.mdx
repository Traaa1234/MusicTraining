# Secondary Dominants

You know the strongest pull in music: a **dominant 7th chord resolving down a
fifth to its tonic**. A **secondary dominant** takes that pull and aims it at a
chord that is *not* the key's tonic — borrowing the power of the V–I motion
for any chord you like.

## Every chord can have its own dominant

In C major, the **V** chord is G, and it resolves to **I**, C. That is the
*primary* dominant.

But there is nothing special about C as a target. *Any* chord can be made to
feel, for a moment, like a tonic — and to do that you put **its own dominant**
in front of it.

Take the **ii** chord, D minor. What is the dominant of D? Count up a fifth
from D and you get **A**; build a dominant 7th and you have **A7**. Place A7
before Dm and it pulls into Dm exactly the way G7 pulls into C:

<PlayButton notes="A3 C#4 E4 G4" label="A7 — the dominant of D minor" chord />
<PlayButton notes="D4 F4 A4" label="...resolving to ii — D minor" chord />

A7 is a **secondary dominant**. For one chord's worth of time, it makes Dm
sound like home.

## The "V of" notation

Secondary dominants are written with a slash: **V7/ii** means "the dominant
seventh *of* the ii chord". You read it "five-seven of two".

So in C major, A7 is **V7/ii**. The slash names the temporary target. Here are
the common secondary dominants of C major:

| Symbol | Chord | Resolves to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| V7/ii | A7 | Dm |
| V7/iii | B7 | Em |
| V7/IV | C7 | F |
| V7/V | D7 | G |
| V7/vi | E7 | Am |

The most common of all is **V7/V** — the dominant of the dominant. In C major
that is **D7**, and it gives the V chord a powerful running start:

<PlayButton notes="D4 F#4 A4 C5" label="V7/V — D7" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G3 B3 D4" label="...resolving to V — G" chord />

## The borrowed leading tone

Look at D7: it contains **F♯**, a note that does not belong to C major. That is
the whole mechanism.

F♯ is the **leading tone of G** — the note a half step below G that strains up
into it. C major has no F♯, so on its own the key cannot pull strongly toward
G. The secondary dominant *imports* that leading tone for a moment, creating a
miniature key-of-G feeling, before the music returns to C.

Every secondary dominant works this way: it brings in one chromatic note — a
borrowed leading tone — to sharpen the pull toward its target. Briefly making a
non-tonic chord feel like home is called **tonicization**.

## Hearing one in context

Drop a secondary dominant into an ordinary progression and the target chord
arrives with extra gravity:

<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="I — C" chord />
<PlayButton notes="A3 C#4 E4 G4" label="V7/ii — A7 (the surprise)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="D4 F4 A4" label="ii — Dm (tonicized)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="G3 B3 D4" label="V — G" chord />

The A7 is the moment of colour — a chord from outside the key that makes the
familiar Dm land harder.

## Why it matters

Secondary dominants are the first step beyond strictly diatonic harmony. They
let you point the strongest pull in music at any chord, adding drama and
forward motion without truly leaving the key. They are everywhere in jazz,
musical theatre and sophisticated pop — and they set up the next lesson, where
that same dominant chord gets *swapped out* entirely.
