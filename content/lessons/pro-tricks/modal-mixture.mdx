# Modal Mixture

The Progressions track introduced **modal interchange** — borrowing a chord
from the parallel key. This lesson takes that idea all the way: the full
palette of borrowed chords, the famous moves they create, and the most elegant
borrowing of all, the Picardy third.

## A reminder, then the full palette

**Modal mixture** (the same thing as modal interchange) borrows chords from the
**parallel key** — the key with the same tonic but the opposite mode. A song in
C major reaches into C minor and takes a chord.

C minor supplies a whole set of chords that C major does not have. Borrow them
into C major and you gain a shadow palette:

| Borrowed chord | In C major | Colour |
| --- | --- | --- |
| iv | F minor | bittersweet, aching |
| ♭VI | A♭ major | dramatic, cinematic |
| ♭VII | B♭ major | rock, anthemic |
| ♭III | E♭ major | bold, surprising lift |
| ii° | D diminished | tense, dark |

Each keeps C as home but smuggles in a flat — a note from the parallel minor.
A single borrowed chord is a splash of a different mode's light.

## The minor iv — the famous one

The most beloved borrowed chord is the **minor iv**. In C major the IV chord is
F major; borrow from C minor and it becomes **F minor**. The move that catches
every ear is **IV → iv → I** — major four sliding into minor four, then home:

<PlayButton notes="F3 A3 C4" label="IV — F major" chord />
<PlayButton notes="F3 Ab3 C4" label="iv — F minor (borrowed)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="I — C major (home)" chord />

The A natural dropping to A♭ is a tiny half-step move that floods the music
with sudden tenderness. It is one of pop's most reliable heartstring-pullers.

## The backdoor and the ♭VII

Borrowing also gives major keys a second route home. The **♭VII** chord — B♭ in
C major — can step straight to I, the **♭VII–I** cadence behind countless rock
anthems. Make it a dominant 7th and you get the **"backdoor" dominant**:

<PlayButton notes="Bb3 D4 F4 Ab4" label="♭VII7 — B♭7 (the backdoor dominant)" chord />
<PlayButton notes="C4 E4 G4" label="I — C major" chord />

It resolves to C without ever using G7 — arriving home, as the name says,
through the back door. And the pairing **♭VI–♭VII–I** — A♭ to B♭ to C — is the
triumphant, cinematic ending of a thousand film scores and power ballads.

## The Picardy third

Mixture works the other direction too — borrowing *from the major* into a minor
key. The classic case is the **Picardy third**: a piece in a **minor** key that
ends on a **major** tonic chord.

<PlayButton notes="A3 C4 E4" label="Minor tonic — A minor" chord />
<PlayButton notes="A3 C#4 E4" label="Picardy third — A major, the surprise ending" chord />

After a whole piece in the dark, the third of the final chord is lifted from
minor to major. The C becomes a C♯, and a sombre work ends in sudden light.
Composers have used it for centuries.

## Mixture is an emotional tool

Notice the common thread. Modal mixture is never about *changing key* — the
tonic never moves. It is about reaching, for a single chord, into the opposite
mode's emotional world: a major key borrowing minor's ache, a minor key
borrowing major's hope. Used sparingly, one borrowed chord can be the most
memorable moment in a song.

## Why it matters

Modal mixture completes your harmonic vocabulary. Diatonic chords, secondary
dominants, substitutions and now borrowed chords give you every colour a
progression can hold. The final lesson is not about *which* chords to play, but
about playing them beautifully — **voice leading**.
