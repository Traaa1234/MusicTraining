# Time Signatures

A piece of music groups its beats into **measures** (also called **bars**). The
**time signature** is the pair of stacked numbers at the start of the music
that tells you exactly how those measures are built.

## Two numbers, two questions

A time signature looks like one number written above another — **4/4**, **3/4**,
**6/8**. Each number answers a different question:

- The **top number** = *how many beats* are in each measure.
- The **bottom number** = *which note value* gets one beat.

The bottom number is a code: **4** means the quarter note is the beat, **8**
means the eighth note is the beat, **2** means the half note is the beat. It is
the same number you would see on the bottom of that note's fraction.

So **3/4** reads as: *three beats per measure, and a quarter note is one beat.*

## 4/4 — common time

By far the most widespread time signature is **4/4**: four beats per measure,
each beat a quarter note. It is so common it is nicknamed **"common time"**.

<PlayButton notes="C4 C4 C4 C4" label="One bar of 4/4 — count 1-2-3-4" />

Count "1-2-3-4, 1-2-3-4" and you are counting in 4/4. The overwhelming majority
of pop, rock and dance music lives here.

## 3/4 and 2/4

Change the top number and you change the grouping:

- **3/4** — three beats per measure. Count "1-2-3". This is **waltz** time, with
  its gentle STRONG-weak-weak lilt.
- **2/4** — two beats per measure. Count "1-2". This is **march** time, with a
  brisk STRONG-weak tread.

| Time signature | Beats per bar | Beat note | Typical feel |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4/4 | 4 | quarter note | pop, rock — "common time" |
| 3/4 | 3 | quarter note | waltz |
| 2/4 | 2 | quarter note | march |
| 6/8 | 6 (felt as 2) | eighth note | rolling, lilting |

## Simple and compound meter

There is one more distinction, and it explains the odd-looking **6/8**.

In **simple** meter, each beat divides naturally into **two**. 4/4, 3/4 and 2/4
are all simple — tap a beat, split it, and you get two even halves.

In **compound** meter, each beat divides into **three**. **6/8** is the common
example. On paper it has six eighth-note beats, but you do not feel six even
pulses — you feel **two** big beats, each one split into three eighths:

`ONE-two-three  FOUR-five-six`

That rolling, galloping triple subdivision is the signature sound of 6/8 — jigs,
many ballads, and a great deal of film music ride on it.

## Why the time signature matters so much

The time signature sets where the **downbeats** fall, and downbeats are where
music places its weight — chord changes, accents, the landing points of
melodies. The very same string of notes counted in 3/4 versus 4/4 produces two
completely different pieces of music, because the strong beats land in
different places.

## Why it matters

The time signature is the frame every rhythm is poured into. It tells you how
to count, where the strong beats are, and how each beat subdivides. With the
frame understood, the final lesson looks at what players actually *do* inside
it — the syncopations, backbeats and swing that turn counting into a groove.
