# Common Rhythmic Patterns

You now have the grid: beat, durations, time signature. This lesson is about
what musicians actually *do* with it. A handful of rhythmic devices —
backbeat, syncopation, triplets and swing — turn a plain count into a
**groove**.

## On the beat and off the beat

First, two words you need. Each beat has two halves. The **onbeat** is the beat
itself — "1", "2", "3", "4". The **offbeat** is the *and* exactly halfway
between — "1-**and**-2-**and**-3-**and**-4-**and**".

Plain, predictable rhythm lands on the onbeats. Everything interesting below
happens because a player puts weight somewhere *other* than there.

## The backbeat

In a 4/4 bar, the natural strong beats are 1 and 3. The **backbeat** flips
that emphasis: it accents beats **2 and 4** instead — usually with a snare drum
crack.

`1  TWO  3  FOUR`

That shift is the heartbeat of rock, pop, soul and funk. Clap along to almost
any pop song and you are clapping the backbeat — beats 2 and 4 — without being
told. It is so natural now that the *un*-accented version sounds old-fashioned.

## Syncopation

**Syncopation** is the broad name for accenting where the ear does *not* expect
it — landing notes on **offbeats**, or on weak beats, instead of the strong
onbeats.

A syncopated rhythm pushes and pulls against the underlying pulse. The beat is
still there, steady underneath, but the notes deliberately dodge it. That
friction between a steady pulse and an off-kilter melody is what makes a rhythm
feel lively rather than mechanical — it is central to funk, reggae, jazz and
Latin music.

## Triplets

Normally a beat splits into **two**. A **triplet** splits it into **three**
equal notes instead — three notes squeezed into the time of two.

You count a triplet "**1**-trip-let, **2**-trip-let". It gives a rolling,
circular feel quite different from the square division of straight eighth
notes, and it is the sound of shuffles, gospel, and a great deal of blues.

## Swing

**Swing** is a specific, hugely important way of bending eighth notes. Instead
of playing each pair of eighths evenly — *equal, equal* — a swung player makes
the first one **long** and the second one **short**:

| Feel | The eighth-note pair | Sounds like |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Straight | even — even | mechanical, precise |
| Swing | long — short | loping, relaxed |

Swing comes from feeling those eighths as if they were the first and third
notes of a triplet. It is the defining rhythmic identity of jazz, and it is why
the same written notes can sound stiff or can sound *alive* depending entirely
on the feel applied to them.

## It all becomes groove

Stack these devices and you get **groove** — the deep, repeating rhythmic
character that makes music move you physically. A backbeat on 2 and 4, a
syncopated bass line dodging the onbeats, a swung feel on the eighths: none of
it is new *notes*, just new *placement*. Groove is proof that rhythm alone, with
no melody at all, can be irresistible.

## Why it matters

These patterns are where rhythm stops being arithmetic and becomes feel. The
grid — beat, durations, time signature — is the same for everyone; what a
musician does inside it is where personality and style live. With this, you
have the core of rhythm: how to count it, write it, and feel it.
