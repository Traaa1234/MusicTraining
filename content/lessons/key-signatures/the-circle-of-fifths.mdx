# The Circle of Fifths

The twelve keys are not a random list. Arrange them by **perfect fifths** and
they fall into a circle — and that circle is the single most useful diagram in
music theory. It is a map of every key signature at once.

## Building the circle

Start at the top with **C major** — the key with no sharps and no flats. Now
move up a **perfect fifth**: C up to G. That gives **G major**, which has one
sharp.

Up another fifth: G to **D** — two sharps. Keep going, one fifth at a time, and
you walk through every sharp key:

`C → G → D → A → E → B → F♯ → C♯`

Each clockwise step adds exactly **one sharp**. Now go the other way from C —
*down* a fifth, which is the same as up a fourth — and you collect flats:

`C → F → B♭ → E♭ → A♭ → D♭ → G♭ → C♭`

Each counter-clockwise step adds exactly **one flat**. Lay all of that around a
clock face and you have the **circle of fifths**:

Click around it and listen — each neighbour is one perfect fifth from the last.

## Why a fifth adds exactly one sharp

This is the elegant part. Why does moving up a fifth change the key signature
by just one note?

Compare the C major scale with the G major scale. They share **six of their
seven notes**. The only difference is that C's F becomes G's **F♯**. Move up a
fifth and you keep almost the whole scale — you just sharpen one note.

So every clockwise step is the smallest possible key change: six notes stay,
one note moves. That is why keys next to each other on the circle sound closely
related, and why a fifth is the natural unit for organising keys.

## The circle closes

Follow the sharps far enough and follow the flats far enough and the two paths
**meet at the bottom** of the circle. F♯ major and G♭ major land on the same
spot — they are **enharmonic**, the same pitches spelled two ways. Twelve
fifths stacked up come back, near enough, to where they began. The circle truly
closes.

## A map of key distance

The circle's real power is that **distance around it equals musical
closeness**:

- **Neighbours** (one step apart) share six of seven notes — moving between
  them sounds smooth and natural.
- **Opposite sides** of the circle share very few notes — moving between them
  sounds dramatic and remote.

A key change to an adjacent key barely ruffles the ear; a jump across the
circle is a bold event. Songwriters use the circle exactly this way — to judge
how far a modulation will *feel*.

## Why it matters

The circle of fifths is a key-signature lookup table, a guide to which keys are
related, a map of smooth chord motion (remember the ii–V–I moved by fifths),
and a writing tool all in one diagram. Next, you will learn to read any key
signature on sight — and the circle is the frame that makes the order of sharps
and flats make sense.
