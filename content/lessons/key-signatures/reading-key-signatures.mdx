# Reading Key Signatures

You can now build key signatures with the circle of fifths. This lesson is
about *reading* them — looking at a cluster of sharps or flats and naming the
key instantly. There are two small patterns and two quick tricks, and then it
is automatic.

## Sharps appear in a fixed order

Sharps are never added randomly. They always appear in the **same order**, the
first one alone, then the first two, then the first three, and so on:

`F♯ – C♯ – G♯ – D♯ – A♯ – E♯ – B♯`

A key with one sharp has F♯. A key with two has F♯ and C♯. A key with three has
F♯, C♯ and G♯. The order never varies.

Notice the order is itself a chain of fifths — F up to C up to G up to D — the
circle of fifths showing up again.

A classic mnemonic for the sharp order is:

> **F**ather **C**harles **G**oes **D**own **A**nd **E**nds **B**attle

## Flats appear in the reverse order

Flats follow the **exact reverse** of the sharp order:

`B♭ – E♭ – A♭ – D♭ – G♭ – C♭ – F♭`

One flat is B♭. Two flats are B♭ and E♭. And so on. The mnemonic simply runs
the sharp sentence backwards:

> **B**attle **E**nds **A**nd **D**own **G**oes **C**harles' **F**ather

| Count | Sharp keys add | Flat keys add |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | F♯ | B♭ |
| 2 | + C♯ | + E♭ |
| 3 | + G♯ | + A♭ |
| 4 | + D♯ | + D♭ |

## Trick 1 — naming a sharp key

To name a **sharp key**, find the **last** sharp in the signature — the
rightmost one — and go **up a half step**. That note is the major key.

- Last sharp is **F♯** → up a half step → **G major**
- Last sharp is **C♯** → up a half step → **D major**
- Last sharp is **G♯** → up a half step → **A major**

The leading tone of any major scale is a half step below the tonic — and the
last sharp added is always that leading tone. So "last sharp plus a half step"
always lands on home.

## Trick 2 — naming a flat key

Flat keys are even easier. The key is the **second-to-last flat** — the name of
the flat one in from the right is the name of the key.

- Flats are B♭, **E♭** → key of **E♭ major**
- Flats are B♭, E♭, **A♭** → key of **A♭ major**
- Flats are B♭, E♭, A♭, **D♭** → key of **D♭ major**

The one exception is **F major**: it has a single flat (B♭), so there is no
"second-to-last". F major you simply memorise — one flat.

## Putting it together

Read a signature in three quick beats:

1. **Sharps or flats?** That tells you which family.
2. **Count them.** That fixes how far around the circle you are.
3. **Apply the trick.** Last sharp up a half step, or second-to-last flat.

See three sharps — F♯, C♯, G♯ — last one is G♯, up a half step is **A major**.
See three flats — B♭, E♭, A♭ — second-to-last is E♭, so **E♭ major**. With a
little practice it takes less than a second.

## Why it matters

Reading a key signature on sight tells you the home note, the scale, and the
seven diatonic chords of the music in front of you — before you play a note.
The last lesson of this track adds the final piece: every signature actually
names *two* keys, one major and one minor, and you will learn how they are
paired.
