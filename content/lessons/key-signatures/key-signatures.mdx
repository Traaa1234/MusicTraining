# Key Signatures

You can build a major scale on any of the twelve notes. The moment you do,
sharps and flats appear — and writing them out over and over quickly becomes a
mess. The **key signature** is music's tidy solution.

## Every key but one needs accidentals

Recall the major scale formula, `W–W–H–W–W–W–H`. Start it on C and it lands on
the white keys with nothing extra. Start it anywhere else and the formula
*forces* black keys.

Build a G major scale and you are required to use one **F♯**:

<Piano scale="G major" />

<PlayButton notes="G3 A3 B3 C4 D4 E4 F#4 G4" label="Hear G major — the F♯ is built in" />

That F♯ is not optional decoration. It is part of what *makes* the scale G
major. And in G major, **every** F is sharp — not just this one.

## The problem with writing it out

Imagine notating a whole song in G major. Every time an F appears on the page
you would have to draw a ♯ in front of it. Dozens, hundreds of times. The page
fills with clutter, and one missed symbol changes the note.

Worse keys make it worse. D♭ major has **five** flats — five symbols to
remember on every relevant note, all song long.

## The solution: state it once

A **key signature** collects all of a key's sharps or flats and places them
**once**, at the very start of every staff line, right after the clef.

It is a standing instruction: "for this whole piece, every F is sharp." The
performer reads it once and applies it throughout. No accidental clutter, no
ambiguity.

So G major's key signature is a single ♯ on the F line. D♭ major's is five
flats. The signature *is* the key's identity, written down.

## Sharp keys and flat keys

A key signature is **all sharps or all flats — never a mix**. This splits the
keys into two families:

| Family | Keys (by number of accidentals) |
| --- | --- |
| No accidentals | C major |
| Sharp keys | G, D, A, E, B, F♯, C♯ major |
| Flat keys | F, B♭, E♭, A♭, D♭, G♭, C♭ major |

C major sits alone in the middle with an empty signature. From there, keys
gather sharps in one direction and flats in the other.

## The same idea for minor keys

Minor keys have key signatures too. As you will see two lessons from now, every
minor key **shares its signature** with one particular major key — A minor, for
instance, has the same empty signature as C major. One signature on the page
can mean either of two keys; context tells you which.

## Why it matters

The key signature is how written music declares "here is the world this piece
lives in" in a single compact symbol. To read it fluently you need two things:
a map of how the keys relate — the **circle of fifths**, next — and the fixed
order the sharps and flats always appear in. The next two lessons cover exactly
those.
