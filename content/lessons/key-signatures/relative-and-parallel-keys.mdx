# Relative & Parallel Keys

A key signature never names just one key. It always names **two** — one major
and one minor — and they are called **relative** keys. This lesson explains
that pairing, and the easily-confused idea next to it: **parallel** keys.

## One signature, two keys

The empty key signature — no sharps, no flats — is the signature of **C
major**. But it is *also* the signature of **A minor**. Both keys use exactly
the same seven notes, the white keys.

That is no accident. You met it back in the Modes track: A minor (Aeolian) is
just the C major scale started from its sixth note. Same notes, different home.
Because the notes are identical, the **key signature is identical** too.

Every key signature works this way — it belongs to a major key and a minor key
at the same time. They are **relative** keys.

## Finding the relative minor

To go from a major key to its **relative minor**, move **down a minor third** —
three half steps — from the major tonic. (That is the same as going *up* to the
sixth degree of the scale.)

- C major → down a minor third → **A minor**
- G major → down a minor third → **E minor**
- F major → down a minor third → **D minor**

<PlayButton notes="C4 A3" label="C down to its relative minor, A" />

To go the other way — minor key to its **relative major** — move **up** a minor
third instead. A minor up a minor third is C major.

| Major key | Relative minor | Shared signature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C major | A minor | none |
| G major | E minor | 1 sharp |
| D major | B minor | 2 sharps |
| F major | D minor | 1 flat |
| B♭ major | G minor | 2 flats |

## Parallel keys — the other pairing

There is a second kind of key relationship, and it is the one beginners mix up
with the first. **Parallel** keys share the **same tonic** but have **opposite
modes**: C major and C **minor** are parallel keys.

Parallel keys do **not** share a key signature. C major has no accidentals; C
minor has three flats. Same home note, different set of notes.

## Don't confuse the two

The two relationships are near-opposites, and the names are the clue:

| | Shared | Different |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Relative** keys | the notes & key signature | the tonic (home note) |
| **Parallel** keys | the tonic (home note) | the notes & key signature |

A simple way to hold them apart:

- **Relative** = *same notes, different home.* C major and A minor.
- **Parallel** = *same home, different notes.* C major and C minor.

## Where you have already met them

Both ideas have appeared before. The bittersweet I–V–**vi**–IV pop loop leans
on vi — the **relative minor** chord — to slip a shadow into a major key. And
**modal interchange** borrows chords from the **parallel** key. Now you have
the proper names: vi taps the relative key; a borrowed chord taps the parallel
one.

## Why it matters

Key relationships are the geography of music. The relative minor gives a
composer a free change of mood with no change of signature; the parallel key
supplies borrowed colour; the circle of fifths charts how far any two keys sit
apart. Together, this track has given you the full map of keys — what they are,
how they are written, and how they connect.
