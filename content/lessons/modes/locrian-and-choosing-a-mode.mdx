# Locrian & Choosing a Mode

One mode is left — the strangest of the seven — and then you will have met all
of them. This lesson finishes the set with **Locrian** and then steps back to
the practical question: faced with seven modes, how do you actually **choose**?

## Locrian — the unstable one

**Locrian** is the natural minor scale with **two** notes lowered: the **2nd**
*and* the **5th**. In C:

`C – D♭ – E♭ – F – G♭ – A♭ – B♭ – C`

<Piano scale="C locrian" />

<PlayButton notes="C4 Db4 Eb4 F4 Gb4 Ab4 Bb4 C5" label="Hear C Locrian" />

It has Phrygian's tense ♭2, but its real oddity is the **lowered 5th**. Every
other mode has a *perfect* fifth above the tonic — the stable, grounding
interval. Locrian replaces it with a **tritone**.

That matters because the chord built on the tonic — root, third, fifth — is no
longer a stable triad. It is a **diminished** chord, which sounds restless and
unresolved:

<PlayButton notes="C4 Eb4 Gb4" label="The Locrian tonic chord — diminished, unsettled" />

A mode needs its tonic to feel like *home*. Locrian's tonic cannot do that — it
will not sit still. For that reason Locrian is **rarely used as a key centre**;
you meet it mostly as a colour passing through, or in some metal. It is worth
knowing, mostly, to complete the picture.

## The brightness spectrum

Here is the idea that ties all seven modes together. Line them up brightest to
darkest and each step **flattens exactly one more note**:

| Mode | Quality | Notes flattened vs. Lydian |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Lydian | major | — (brightest) |
| Ionian | major | ♯4 → 4 |
| Mixolydian | major | + ♭7 |
| Dorian | minor | + ♭3 |
| Aeolian | minor | + ♭6 |
| Phrygian | minor | + ♭2 |
| Locrian | diminished | + ♭5 (darkest) |

Seven modes are not seven things to memorise. They are **one dimmer switch**.
Slide it up for light, down for dark — and each click moves a single note.

## How to choose a mode

In practice, picking a mode comes down to three questions:

1. **Major or minor?** Decide the basic mood first. Major third → Lydian,
   Ionian or Mixolydian. Minor third → Dorian, Aeolian, Phrygian or Locrian.
2. **How bright?** Within that family, slide along the spectrum. Want the
   major family but a little edgier? Mixolydian. Want minor but hopeful?
   Dorian.
3. **Lean on the characteristic note.** This is what actually makes a mode
   audible. Each mode lives or dies by one note:

| Mode | Characteristic note |
| --- | --- |
| Lydian | raised 4th |
| Mixolydian | lowered 7th |
| Dorian | raised 6th |
| Phrygian | lowered 2nd |
| Locrian | lowered 5th |

## Make the tonic stick

None of this works without the lesson from the very first mode: a mode only
exists when its **tonic is genuinely home**. Modal music almost always sits on
a **static anchor** — a drone, a single repeating chord, a vamp that never
resolves away. Take that anchor away and the ear slides back to the parent
major scale, and the mode evaporates.

So the real recipe is small: pick your mood on the spectrum, hold the tonic
down with a drone or vamp, and lean on the characteristic note. Do those three
things and any of the seven modes is yours.

## Why it matters

With modes finished, you can draw **seven distinct colours** from a single
parent scale. That is the complete melodic palette — and from here the lessons
turn to stacking these notes into **chords**, where the same characteristic
notes you just learned become the colour tones of rich harmony.
