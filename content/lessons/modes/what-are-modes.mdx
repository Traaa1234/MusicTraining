# What Are Modes?

Modes have a reputation for being hard. They are not. A mode is just a major
scale **started from a different note** — and once that one idea clicks, all
seven modes fall into place at once.

## One scale, seven starting points

Take the C major scale — the white keys, C D E F G A B. Now play those same
seven notes, but start and end on **D** instead:

`D – E – F – G – A – B – C – D`

Same keys. Same notes. But the music now orbits **D**, not C. The pattern of
whole and half steps you walk through is different, so it *sounds* different —
and that new sound is a **mode**.

Do this from each of the seven white keys and you get the seven modes:

| Start on | Mode | Also known as |
| --- | --- | --- |
| C | Ionian | the major scale |
| D | Dorian | — |
| E | Phrygian | — |
| F | Lydian | — |
| G | Mixolydian | — |
| A | Aeolian | the natural minor scale |
| B | Locrian | — |

## You already know two of them

Look at that table again. **Ionian is the major scale** you spent a whole
lesson on. **Aeolian is the natural minor scale.** You already own two of the
seven modes — modes are not new notes, just a new way of organising notes you
have used all along.

## The parent scale

The scale all seven modes are drawn from is called their **parent scale**. C
major is the parent of all the modes above.

Here is the parent, C major:

<Piano scale="C major" />

And here is D Dorian — start the same white keys from D:

<Piano scale="D dorian" />

The highlighted keys are identical. The only thing that changed is **which note
is home**.

## Home is everything

This is the part beginners miss. Playing the notes D-E-F-G-A-B-C-D does **not**
automatically give you Dorian. If a C major chord is droning underneath, your
ear still hears C as home — you have just played C major starting on a
different note.

A mode only exists when its tonic genuinely *is* the centre of gravity. You
make that happen with context: a held bass note, a repeating chord, landing on
and returning to that tonic.

<PlayButton notes="D3 D4 E4 F4 G4 A4 B4 C5 D5" label="D as a drone — now it is Dorian" />

So a mode is two things together: a **set of notes** (from the parent scale)
and a **chosen home** (the tonic). Change the home, change the mode.

## The characteristic note

If six of the seven modes share notes with each other, what makes each one
recognisable? Each mode has **one note** — its **characteristic note** — that
is the single biggest clue to its identity. Lean on that note and the mode
sounds like itself; avoid it and the mode goes blurry.

The next three lessons take the five modes you do not yet know — Lydian,
Mixolydian, Dorian, Phrygian and Locrian — and for each one pin down that
characteristic note and the mood it creates.

## Why it matters

Modes are how you get **seven different moods out of one set of notes**. That
is an enormous amount of expressive range for very little new memorising — and
it is the foundation of everything from Celtic folk to jazz to film scoring.
