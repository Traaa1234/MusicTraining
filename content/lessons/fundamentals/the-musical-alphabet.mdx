# The Musical Alphabet & Octaves

Before you can talk about scales, chords, or keys, you need names for the
notes. Music uses a surprisingly small set of them — and once you see the
pattern, the whole keyboard stops looking like a wall of keys.

## Seven letters, then it repeats

Western music names notes with just **seven letters**:

**A — B — C — D — E — F — G**

After G you don't continue to H. You start over at A. The musical alphabet is
a **loop, not a line** — it cycles round and round, as low or as high as the
instrument can reach.

That's the whole naming system. Seven letters. Every sharp, every flat, every
chord symbol you will ever meet is built on top of these seven.

## The keyboard makes it obvious

A piano keyboard is the clearest picture of this loop. Look only at the
**white keys**: they are simply A B C D E F G, A B C D E F G, over and over.

<Piano />

Notice the black keys come in groups of **two** and **three**. That pattern
is your map. The white key immediately to the **left of any group of two**
black keys is always **C**. Find the twos, find C, and you can name every
other white key by counting letters from there.

## What is an octave?

Play a C, then play the very next C above it. They sound different — one is
higher — and yet they sound like *the same note*. That distance, from one
note up to the next note of the same name, is an **octave**.

<PlayButton notes="C3 C4" label="Hear an octave" />

The word comes from the Latin *octo*, eight: count the white keys
C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C and the second C is the eighth note you land on.

## Why octaves sound "the same"

This isn't your imagination — it's physics. When you play a note one octave
higher, the string or air column vibrates **exactly twice as fast**. A 2:1
ratio is the simplest possible relationship between two pitches, so your ear
fuses them: same note, just brighter.

Because of this, musicians treat every C on the keyboard as one shared idea —
"C" — and only mention the octave when it actually matters.

## Naming the octaves

When the octave *does* matter, we tag the letter with a number. The standard
reference point is **middle C, written C4** — roughly the middle of a piano.

| Note   | Roughly where             |
| ------ | ------------------------- |
| C2     | low, left-hand bass       |
| C3     | just below middle C       |
| **C4** | **middle C**              |
| C5     | a comfortable singing C   |
| A4     | 440 Hz — the tuning pitch |

So "C4" means "the C in the fourth octave." The number ticks over at **C**,
not at A: C4 up through B4 are all octave 4, and the next note, C5, begins
octave 5.

## Why it matters

Seven letters plus an octave number let you name **any** key on **any**
instrument, exactly and unambiguously. Every scale and chord in the lessons
ahead is just a chosen handful of these names. Get this loop solid and the
rest of theory finally has something to stand on.
