# Sharps, Flats & Enharmonics

The seven letters name the white keys. But there are black keys too — five of
them in every octave — and they need names. That's the job of **sharps** and
**flats**.

## The black keys need names

Count the black keys inside one octave and you'll find **five**. Western
music never handed them their own letters. Instead, each black key is named
by its relationship to a neighbouring white key.

<Piano />

## Sharp = up, flat = down

Two small symbols do all the work:

- A **sharp (♯)** raises a note by one half step — the very next key up.
- A **flat (♭)** lowers a note by one half step — the very next key down.

So the black key sitting between C and D can be reached two ways:

- starting from **C**, go **up** a half step → **C♯**
- starting from **D**, go **down** a half step → **D♭**

<PlayButton notes="C4 C#4" label="C up to C♯" />
<PlayButton notes="D4 Db4" label="D down to D♭" />

Play both. Either path lands you on the **same black key**.

## Enharmonic equivalents

That is the heart of this lesson. **C♯ and D♭ are the same pitch with two
different names.** Two names for one identical sound are called **enharmonic
equivalents**.

Every black key carries a pair of names:

| Sharp name | Flat name |
| ---------- | --------- |
| C♯         | D♭        |
| D♯         | E♭        |
| F♯         | G♭        |
| G♯         | A♭        |
| A♯         | B♭        |

## So which name do you use?

If they sound identical, why keep two names? **Spelling.** Music is written
so that a scale or chord uses each letter once and reads cleanly on the page.
In one key a black key is correctly an **F♯**; in another it is a **G♭** —
same sound, different *context*. You'll learn which is which as you go. For
now, the takeaway is simple: both names point at one key.

## The two "missing" black keys

Look at the keyboard again. Between **E and F**, and between **B and C**,
there is **no black key at all**. Those white keys are already just a half
step apart.

This has a neat consequence. "E♯" — E raised a half step — lands on **F**
itself. And "C♭" lands on **B**. You will rarely *write* notes that way, but
it explains why E-to-F and B-to-C feel different from every other pair of
neighbouring letters.

## The natural sign

One more symbol completes the set: the **natural (♮)**. It cancels a sharp or
flat and returns a note to its plain white-key self. If a piece has been
turning every F into F♯, a natural sign says "this F — play it plain."

## Why it matters

Sharps and flats unlock the other five pitches of the twelve. With the white
keys *and* the black keys named, you can now spell **all twelve notes** —
and, as you'll see, every scale and chord is just a chosen handful drawn from
those twelve.
