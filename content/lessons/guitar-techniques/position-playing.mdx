# Position Playing

Pentatonic boxes carved the neck into five regions for a five-note scale.
**Position playing** applies the same idea to the full seven-note scale — and
it is the habit that lets a guitarist play anywhere on the fretboard
efficiently and without getting lost.

## What a position is

A **position** is a region of the neck — about four or five frets wide — where
the fretting hand **stays put**. Each finger takes roughly one fret: index,
middle, ring and little finger covering four adjacent frets.

To play **in position** means to play a passage using all six strings within
that one fret span, **without shifting the hand** up or down the neck. The
hand is an anchor; the fingers do all the reaching.

## The major scale in positions

You already know the C major scale as a line of notes. Spread it across all six
strings and it covers the whole fretboard:

<Fretboard scale="C major" />

That full map breaks into **playable positions** — and just like the pentatonic
boxes, the major scale has **five CAGED positions**, each a hand-sized shape
rooted in one of the C, A, G, E and D forms. Learn the five and you can play
the scale in any region of the neck.

## Three notes per string

There is a second, very popular way to organise the major scale: the
**three-notes-per-string** system.

Instead of two-or-three notes per string in mixed amounts, every string gets
**exactly three notes**. This produces **seven** patterns — one starting from
each degree of the scale, which means one for each of the seven modes.

Three-notes-per-string shapes are even and predictable, and that regularity
makes them excellent for **speed and legato** playing — fast runs flow
naturally when each string has the same number of notes.

| System | Patterns | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| CAGED positions | 5 | linking scales to chord shapes |
| Three-notes-per-string | 7 | speed, even legato runs |

Neither is "correct" — most players learn both and switch between them.

## Shifting between positions

Staying in one position is efficient, but no single position reaches the whole
neck. To travel, you **shift** — move the whole hand to a new position.

A clean **position shift** happens on a single, deliberate note: one finger
slides or steps to the new region, and the hand resettles there. Done well, the
shift is invisible — the listener hears one flowing line, not a hand jumping.

The skill is choosing **where** to shift. Good players shift on a long note, a
rest, or a natural phrase break — never in the middle of a fast burst, where a
lurch would show.

## Why play in position at all

Position playing gives you three things:

- **Efficiency** — minimal hand movement, so passages are cleaner and faster.
- **Reliability** — the shapes are the same in every key; you move the shape,
  not relearn the notes.
- **Whole-neck freedom** — five (or seven) positions overlap to cover every
  fret, so you are never stuck in one spot.

## Why it matters

Position playing is how a guitarist turns scattered fretboard knowledge into
fluent movement — the right notes, anywhere, with the least effort. With the
CAGED chords, the pentatonic boxes and now full-scale positions, you have the
complete map of the neck. The final lesson covers how to make those notes
**expressive**: the essential playing techniques.
