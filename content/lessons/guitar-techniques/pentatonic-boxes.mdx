# Pentatonic Boxes

The minor pentatonic is the most-played scale in rock, blues and pop lead
guitar. On the fretboard it organises itself into **five connected shapes** —
the **pentatonic boxes** — and learning them turns the whole neck into familiar
territory.

## Five notes, five boxes

The minor pentatonic has five notes. Spread those five notes across all six
strings and twelve frets and a pattern emerges: the scale repeats in **five
fingering shapes**, each sitting in its own region of the neck.

Here is the A minor pentatonic — A, C, D, E, G — laid out across the fretboard:

<Fretboard scale="A minor-pentatonic" />

Every note you see belongs to the scale. A **box** is simply one playable
chunk of that map — a shape your hand can cover without moving.

## Box 1 — the one everyone learns first

The most famous shape is **Box 1**. For A minor pentatonic it sits around the
5th fret, and it is compact, symmetrical and easy to memorise — two notes on
every string.

<ChordDiagram chord="A minor" />

It is the first scale shape most lead guitarists ever learn, and an enormous
amount of blues and rock soloing never leaves it. Master Box 1 and you can
already improvise.

## The boxes connect to CAGED

The five boxes are not five unrelated patterns. Each one lines up with a
**CAGED shape** from the last lesson — the same five shapes, the same cycle up
the neck. Box 1 overlaps Box 2, Box 2 overlaps Box 3, and so on around the
neck and back again.

Because the boxes **interlock**, the top two strings of one box are the bottom
two strings of the next. Learn where they overlap and you can slide from any
box into its neighbour without a gap.

| Box | Built around | Region (for A minor pentatonic) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Box 1 | E shape | around fret 5 |
| Box 2 | D shape | around fret 7 |
| Box 3 | C shape | around fret 10 |
| Box 4 | A shape | around fret 12 |
| Box 5 | G shape | around fret 2 |

## Major pentatonic — the same shapes

Here is the bonus. The **major pentatonic** uses the **exact same five box
shapes**. You met why in the Scales track: a major pentatonic and a minor
pentatonic three frets lower share the same five notes — they are relatives.

So A minor pentatonic Box 1 *is* C major pentatonic — same shape, same frets.
You only relabel which note is "home". Learn the five boxes once and you own
both scales.

## Practising the boxes

A reliable path:

1. **Memorise Box 1** until it is automatic — ascending and descending.
2. **Add Box 2**, directly above it, and practise crossing the overlap.
3. **Connect them** — run a lick that starts in Box 1 and ends in Box 2.
4. Add the remaining boxes the same way, always through the overlaps.

The goal is not five separate cages but **one continuous scale** that happens
to be easy to grab anywhere.

## Why it matters

The pentatonic boxes are the practical lead-guitar map. They let you find the
right notes instantly in any region of the neck, in any key, for both the major
and minor pentatonic. Next, the lessons widen this idea from the five-note
pentatonic to the full seven-note scale, with **position playing**.
